ACQUISITION PROBABILITY MODEL - HOW I USED THE RESEARCH
Version 0.3 | Notes updated 30 September 2026

Why I looked at research

I wanted the model's inputs to have a financial reason behind them, rather than
just adding variables and hoping they improved the results. I used these papers
as starting points for choosing inputs and deciding how to test the model.

I did not recreate the papers' original datasets or methods. This is a smaller
student project that adapts some of their ideas. The demo uses generated data,
so its results are not evidence that the model can predict real takeovers.

How I set up the model

1. I mark whether a company receives a qualifying takeover announcement in the
   next 12 months. I leave out observations without a full year of follow-up.
2. I keep the actual mix of targets and non-targets in validation and testing,
   rather than creating an artificially balanced sample.
3. I compare companies with peers on the same date. Missing-value filling and
   other data preparation learn from the training data only.
4. I compare four models on later years. Each fit only uses outcomes that would
   already have been known at that point, so it cannot learn from the future.
5. I use a separate sample to adjust scores into estimated probabilities, then
   test the chosen model without changing it using the final test results.
6. I focus on acquisition frequency in the top-ranked 10%, compared with the
   full sample. I also check how many targets are captured and how accurate the
   probabilities are.

The papers and what I changed

1. Testing a model when takeovers are rare

Palepu (1986), Predicting takeover targets: A methodological and empirical analysis.
https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-4101(86)90008-X

This paper helped shape how I think about the sample and the test. High accuracy
can be misleading when most companies are not acquired. I keep all eligible
companies in the evaluation and measure whether the highest-ranked group has
more takeovers than the full sample.

During training, I give the rarer takeover observations more weight. I do not
change the event mix in validation or testing. I also keep probability adjustment
separate from model selection. Those timing safeguards are my implementation
choices, not a claim that this paper used the exact same approach.
Code: modeling.py, metrics.py and labels.py.

2. Industry activity and financing conditions

Mitchell and Mulherin (1996), The impact of industry shocks on takeover and
restructuring activity. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(95)00860-H
Harford (2005), What drives merger waves?
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304405X04002041

These papers led me to look beyond the individual company. I added a comparison
of recent industry deal activity with its three-year average, the size of changes
in industry sales, and credit spreads as a rough measure of financing conditions.
I also combine the industry-change measure with the financing measure.

I keep the existing measures of industry concentration and past deal activity.
My added variables are simple stand-ins for these ideas, not the exact measures
used in the papers.
Code: features.py, the industry_waves group, and sql/feature_mart.sql.

3. Ownership, assets and takeover defenses

Ambrose and Megginson (1992), The role of asset structure, ownership structure,
and takeover defenses in determining acquisition likelihood.
https://doi.org/10.2307/2331141

I added changes in institutional ownership, stakes held by large outside
shareholders, takeover-defense indicators, and property, plant and equipment
as a share of total assets. If I do not have a governance value, I leave it
missing instead of assuming that the company has no takeover defense.

I do not assume these variables always affect takeover likelihood in the same
direction. The paper's significant defense finding concerned blank-check
preferred stock. My poison-pill and staggered-board inputs are related ideas
to test, not a recreation of that particular finding.
Code: features.py, the ownership_and_assets group, and data.py.

4. Cheap valuations and weak stock performance

Edmans, Goldstein and Jiang (2012), The real effects of financial markets:
The impact of prices on takeovers. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2012.01738.x
Agrawal and Jaffe (2003), Do takeover targets underperform? Evidence from
operating and stock returns. https://doi.org/10.2307/4126741

I did not want to assume that takeover targets are always struggling companies.
I compare valuation and stock returns with industry peers, and include a term
that combines a valuation discount with weak relative stock performance.

I then test a version without the stock-return terms to see whether they help.
This can show a predictive relationship, but it cannot prove that a low share
price causes a takeover. I also test free cash flow relative to enterprise value
and cash combined with falling sales. These are extra project ideas, not results
copied from the papers.
Code: features.py and the model comparisons in modeling.py.

5. Innovation and strategic assets

Wu and Chung (2019), Corporate innovation, likelihood to be acquired, and
takeover premiums. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbankfin.2019.105634

I added R&D spending relative to revenue, patent counts on a log scale, and
citations per patent. The important rule is that patent information must have
been available on the observation date. Using citations received years later
would give the model information it could not have known at the time.

Real historical patent data still needs to be checked before it reaches the
model. I do not estimate the premium a buyer might pay.
Code: features.py, the innovation group.

6. Looking beyond accuracy

Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015), The precision-recall plot is more informative than
the ROC plot when evaluating binary classifiers on imbalanced datasets.
https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0118432

I include average precision alongside lift and target capture. This gives me
another way to check how well the model ranks the rare takeover observations.
I also keep Brier score and log loss, which measure errors in the probabilities.

For the lift uncertainty interval, I repeatedly sample whole company histories.
That keeps a company's observations together across years. This is a separate
project choice, not a method I attribute to this paper. The interval does not
cover every risk, such as a different market environment or missing deal records.
Code: metrics.py.

7. A good shortlist is not the same as a profitable strategy

Cremers, Nair and John (2009), Takeovers and the cross-section of returns.
https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1365691

I keep takeover prediction separate from investment performance. Finding more
future targets does not tell me what return I would earn after buying them.
I would also need to consider entry prices, waiting time, trading costs and the
companies that never receive a bid. I do not claim a trading return or Sharpe
ratio from the classification results.

What I left out to keep the project manageable

Hoberg and Phillips (2010), Product market synergies and competition in mergers
and acquisitions: A text-based analysis. https://www.nber.org/papers/w14289
Powell (1997), Modelling takeover likelihood. https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-5957.00148

Comparing companies' product descriptions and studying different types of
takeovers are useful directions for future work. I removed the optional text
comparison and deal-type reports from this version to keep the focus on the
financial inputs. Both would need more historical data to do properly. The
current model does not predict separate probabilities for different deal types.

I also removed automatic feature-importance calculations, duplicate CSV and
database exports, extra charts and repeated documentation. I kept the four
model comparisons and the checks that prevent future information from entering
training. Each run saves five CSV reports, a JSON summary, a model and one chart.
The demo also saves its generated input data.

Where to find the work

All Python files below are in src/acq_probability/.
- pipeline.py brings the steps together and saves the results.
- features.py calculates 17 basic numeric inputs and 15 research additions,
  with sector included as a separate category.
- modeling.py prepares the data, compares models and adjusts their probabilities.
- metrics.py calculates the ranking results and uncertainty interval.
- labels.py creates the takeover labels; data.py checks the input data.
- reporting.py saves the JSON summary and the chart.

The commands are demo, label, train and score. The SQL backtest reads the
predictions CSV directly, so I do not need to maintain a separate database.

How I check it and what the demo tells me

With the virtual environment active, run:
    pytest
    ruff check .
    acq-model demo

The tests check the calculations, date rules, missing inputs, saved-model scoring,
and agreement between Python and SQL. They also check that calibration and final
test outcomes cannot influence which model is selected.

The demo starts with 650 fictional companies and uses random seed 42. The basic
logistic model wins model selection. In the final test, 18 of 194 shortlisted
company-year observations have an announcement, compared with 46 of 1,930 overall.
That is 9.28% versus 2.38%, or 3.89x lift and 39.1% target capture.

There is still a clear limitation: the average predicted probability is 4.02%,
above the actual 2.38% event rate. A useful ranking does not mean the probabilities
are accurate, and only 19 events are available for the probability-adjustment step.

My next step would be to test the same approach on real historical company data,
including acquired and delisted firms. I would need reliable announcement records
and dates showing when each input became available. If I change the model after
looking at the test results, I would need a fresh test period before making a
new claim about its performance.
